IB Physics Higher Level 12 is the second year of a two-year course aimed at enhancing the understanding and 
application of physics concepts and principles. The course uses a student-centered, inquiry-based approach to study 
various physical phenomena. Students will analyze and process data to determine relationships between physical 
variables, leading to logical conclusions about these relationships. Besides scientific investigation, students will 
engage in problem-solving activities that involve critical thinking and the basic application of physics formulas.

Homework assignments will be given each time there is class. In most cases, students will have time to 
complete the homework in class. Answers to questions will be provided the following class with the intent 
that students will reflect on why answers were correct, partially correct, or incorrect. 
Laboratory experiments will often be done at the beginning of a unit. The goal is for students to make 
connections between quantities – specifically the independent and dependent variables under investigation. 
Formative assessments will be given every class and vary in nature. The goal is for students is to rehearse 
and reflect on their learning to improve on the learning outcome. 
Summative assessments will be given at the end of a unit. 
Class structure (students can expect the following): 
(1) Question and answer session on any homework assigned.
(2) Engagement session – A brief five-minute introduction to a concept that motivates thinking about
the phenomenon.
(3) Exploration – A lab activity involving the collection of data from an online simulation or through
the use of laboratory equipment.
(4) Explanation – A ten to twenty minute explanation of the concepts and material under investigation.
Students will be expected to take notes. Various types of formative assessments will be utilized to
help students improve and reflect on the learning outcomes.
(5) Elaboration – Students will be assigned conceptual questions or computational questions with
regards to the physics principles under investigation.

\n--- PAGE 22 ---\nThe nature of electromagnetic waves
● They are regarded as _______________________.
● They can travel through a vacuum.
● They have an identical ____________________________.
● They consist of a time-varying electric field at right angles
to a time-varying magnetic field. The strengths of these
fields have their maximum value at the same time.
● They arise from the motion of accelerated electrons (or
other charged particles) or occur when charged particles

\n--- PAGE 76 ---\nD.2 Life Cycle of a Star - Worksheet 
 
A STAR IS BORN – STAGES COMMON TO ALL STARS 
All stars start as a nebula.  A nebula is a large cloud of gas and dust. Gravity can pull 
some of the gas and dust in a nebula together. The contracting cloud is then called a 
protostar. A protostar is the earliest stage of a star’s life. A star is born when the gas 
and dust from a nebula become so hot that nuclear fusion starts. Gravitational energy 
has changed to kinetic energy for this to happen. Once a star has “turned on” it is known 
as a main sequence star.  When a main sequence star begins to run out of hydrogen fuel 
in its core(when it has used about 12% of its total hydrogen), the star becomes a red 
giant or a red super giant. 
THE DEATH OF A LOW OR MEDIUM MASS STAR 
After a  low or medium mass or star has become a red giant the outer parts grow bigger 
and drift into space, forming a cloud of gas called a planetary nebula. The blue-white 
hot core of the star that is left behind cools and becomes a white dwarf (maximum mass 
1.4 solar masses).  The white dwarf eventually runs out of fuel and dies as a black 
dwarf. 
 
THE DEATH OF A HIGH MASS STAR 
A red super giant can fuse together larger and larger atoms until it makes iron. The star 
then suddenly explodes. The explosion is called a supernova. After the star explodes, 
some of the materials from the star are left behind. This material may form a neutron star. 
Neutron stars (max mass 3 solar masses) are the remains of high-mass stars. The most 
massive stars (remnant bigger than 3 solar masses) become black holes when they die. 
The gravity of the black hole is so strong that nothing can escape, not even light.

Heat cycles and
Heat engines
A device that performs useful work by continuously converting energy to work is known as a heat
engine.
The first description of a cyclic heat engine was given by the French engineer and physicist Nicolas
Léonard Sadi Carnot in 1824. He described a cycle in which an ideal gas is carried around four
processes in sequence: two isothermal and two adiabatic. The moving parts of the heat engine
are assumed to be completely frictionless. This is known as theCarnot cycle.
AB → isothermal expansion
BC → adiabatic expansion
CD → isothermal compression (work done on the gas)
DA → adiabatic compression (work done on the gas)
91
\n--- PAGE 92 ---\nReversible nature
of the Carnot
cycle.
A heat engine that performs the Carnot cycle is reversible.
A reversible process is one in which a system can be returned to its previous state with only an
infinitesimal change to the properties of the system or its surroundings. In order for this to be
possible it would have to go at an infinitely slow pace.
The Carnot cycle is important because it represents the optimal efficiency possible for a heat
engine. For all real world engines, the cycle is not reversible. There is always energy lost to
friction or the environment.

The two 
postulates of 
special relativity 
1. The laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames of reference.  
2. The speed of light relative to any observer in an inertial frame is independent of the 
motion of the source of light relative to the inertial observer. 
Simultaneity Classical (Newtonian) Simultaneity: 
● In classical physics, time is considered absolute, meaning that all observers, regardless of 
their state of motion, will agree on whether two events occur simultaneously. 
● This implies a universal clock that ticks the same for everyone, making simultaneity an 
objective fact. 
Relativistic Simultaneity: 
● In the theory of relativity, simultaneity is relative, depending on the observer's state of 
motion. 
● Two events that are simultaneous in one frame of reference may not be simultaneous in 
another frame moving relative to the first. 
● This is due to the finite speed of light and the way space and time are intertwined in the 
fabric of spacetime.
